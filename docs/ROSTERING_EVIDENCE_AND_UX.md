# Evidence-informed rostering UX

## Evidence synthesis

Recent healthcare rostering research consistently identifies the following user needs:

- fairness and equity in allocation
- meaningful employee participation
- flexibility and autonomy
- transparency about why a roster decision was made
- support for work-life balance
- fatigue-aware scheduling
- effective handling of short-notice absence
- simple shift swapping/self-scheduling with safeguards
- human oversight when algorithmic recommendations are used

Electronic/self-rostering has generally shown positive effects for healthcare workers and organisations, including satisfaction, control, work-life balance and retention, but evidence also identifies risks such as inequity, overtime and excessive shift-change requests.

## Product implications

The application should therefore NOT optimise only for staffing coverage.

Every scheduling recommendation should balance:

1. Hard constraints — legal/operational/competence conflicts that must not be violated.
2. Safety constraints — fatigue, insufficient rest and other high-risk patterns.
3. Fairness — equitable distribution of nights, weekends, unpopular shifts and workload.
4. Employee preferences — availability, preferred shifts and requested days off.
5. Operational efficiency — coverage, competence mix and FTE utilisation.

Recommendations must remain explainable and editable by the responsible human user.

## UX principles

### The next action should be obvious
The dashboard should surface uncovered or at-risk shifts first and provide a direct action such as `Find candidate`.

### Show reasons, not unexplained scores
A recommendation should explain positive and negative factors. Example:

- Required competence: met
- Availability: met
- Existing assignment conflict: none
- Weekend load: low
- Preference: preferred
- Rest warning: none

### Make fairness visible
Users should be able to see workload indicators and why one employee is recommended over another. Avoid opaque ranking that can be perceived as favouritism.

### Preserve human control
Automation should recommend and simulate alternatives. Final responsibility remains with the authorised scheduling user.

### Design for exceptions
Short-notice absence, shift swaps and unexpected staffing gaps should be first-class workflows rather than workarounds.

## Planned product metrics

We should measure:

- time required to fill a vacant shift
- number of manual edits per roster
- percentage of shifts covered on first publication
- preference satisfaction
- weekend/night distribution variance
- overtime generated by scheduling
- number of preventable scheduling conflicts
- user-reported ease of use
- user trust in recommendations

## Research limitation

The literature provides evidence about common rostering problems and useful design principles. It does not prove that these are the exact problems experienced by users of this application. Local user research and usability testing must therefore be used before claiming that a design is optimal.
